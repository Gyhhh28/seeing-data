# The final data story
https://carnegiemellon.shorthandstories.com/the-fever-effect/index.html
For my final project, I created a Shorthand story called The Fever Effect. I looked at WNBA attendance from 2023 to 2025, especially what happened when the Indiana Fever played on the road after Caitlin Clark joined the team in 2024.

I wanted to find out whether Indiana was really drawing unusually large crowds, or whether it was simply part of the overall growth of the WNBA. The story starts with the bigger picture, then looks at Indiana's road games, how attendance differed across cities, and whether the pattern continued in 2025.

# Changes made since Part II
After finishing Part II, I made quite a few changes to both the story and the visuals. One of the biggest changes was the order of the story. At first, I wanted to focus directly on the Indiana Fever's road attendance. But after getting feedback, I realized that readers needed to understand what was happening across the WNBA first. So I decided to start with the overall attendance growth before introducing Indiana as an outlier.

I also changed how I presented the comparisons between different cities. In my earlier design, some readers found the market-level chart confusing, especially because it wasn't clear what Indiana's attendance was being compared against. For the final version, I compared each team's typical home attendance with attendance when Indiana visited. I think this makes the difference much easier to see without having to explain too much.

Another change was the overall visual style. My original wireframes were mostly focused on charts, but I wanted the final story to feel more like a sports feature. I ended up using larger headlines, basketball imagery, and fewer words on each screen. I also added a section showing how some Fever games were moved to bigger arenas, which helped connect the attendance numbers to something that actually happened.

## The audience
My target audience is general sports fans, especially people who don't follow the WNBA closely. I wanted the story to be interesting even for someone who knows very little about women's basketball.

For Part II, I got feedback from three people with different backgrounds: one who knew almost nothing about basketball, one who watched the NBA but didn't really follow the WNBA, and one who was more familiar with other sports. I wanted to see whether people with different levels of sports knowledge could understand the same story.

One thing I noticed was that some comparisons were harder to understand than I expected. For example, the market-level chart wasn't very clear to everyone, and some readers weren't sure what the numbers were being compared to. Based on that feedback, I simplified the chart and made the comparison more direct. I also tried to keep the text short and let the visuals explain most of the story.

I think these changes made the final version easier to follow, especially for people who aren't already familiar with the WNBA.

## Final design decisions
For the final design, I wanted something that looked more like a sports story than a typical data dashboard. I took inspiration from sports websites like ESPN, especially their use of large headlines, full-screen images, and bold colors. Since the story focuses on Indiana, I used red, yellow, and dark navy as the main colors.

I also tried to use different types of visuals instead of repeating the same chart throughout the story. For example, I used bar charts to show how Indiana's road attendance ranking changed, and a connected-dot chart to compare attendance across different cities. I included a section about games moving to larger arenas with photos rather than another chart, because I felt the story needed a visual break.

One thing I struggled with was getting everything to fit properly in Shorthand. Some of my charts looked fine as images, but parts of the text or data got cut off when I uploaded them as full-screen backgrounds. I had to go back and adjust the font sizes, spacing, and chart layouts several times. It was frustrating, but it also made me realize that designing a visualization isn't just about making it look good. It also needs to work in the format where people will actually see it.
